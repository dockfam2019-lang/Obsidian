## Block A — Lines 1–490  
### Opening → Heorot / Grendel / voyage / coast-guard / credentials

**What happens**

The poem opens not with Beowulf but with a founder-king of the Danes, **Shield Sheafson**: a kid who arrives from nowhere, grows into a terrifying conqueror and generous “ring-giver,” then gets a fancy ship-funeral. That prologue teaches the yardstick for good kingship — protect people, give gold, get remembered — before the main plot starts.

Shield’s line leads to **Hrothgar**, who builds a glorious mead-hall called **Heorot** (the “hart” hall). There is music, gift-giving, and a Creation song. Joy in the hall creates its opposite: a border-monster, **Grendel**, hears the harp and hates it. The narrator ties Grendel to **Cain’s** cursed line — so he’s both a Germanic feud-creature and a Christian “outcast from God.”

For twelve winters Grendel raids at night, killing and eating warriors. He takes over Heorot after dark. No one can pay him off (no *wergild* / death-price works). The Danes sometimes pray at pagan shrines; the narrator scolds that as hopeless. Hrothgar’s power looks broken: a ring-giver who can’t keep the benches safe.

Across the sea, **Hygelac’s thane Beowulf** hears about Grendel, gathers fourteen companions, and sails to Denmark. A **coast-guard** challenges them — who’s this armed stranger? Beowulf answers with reputation and purpose. They march to Heorot; court officer **Wulfgar** gets them an audience.

Before Hrothgar, Beowulf offers to fight Grendel **hand-to-hand**, no weapons (Grendel’s hide supposedly shrugs off blades anyway). Hrothgar remembers helping Beowulf’s father Ecgtheow in an old feud, so welcoming Beowulf also settles a debt of hospitality. A public boast (*beot*) is locked in: tomorrow morning will prove whether words match deeds.

**Voice note (light):** Early pages braid two tones — oral “we have heard of fame” storytelling, and a Christian narrator who keeps baptizing the same scenes (God sends the founder-child; Cain explains the monster; pagan shrines = wrong prayer). You don’t have to pick one; the poem stacks both.

## Block B — Lines 662–835  
### Hrothgar leaves → Beowulf beds → Grendel fight → arm trophy

**What happens**

Night of the fight: Hrothgar and Wealhtheow leave the hall. Beowulf **takes off his armor and puts aside his sword**, matching Grendel’s supposed weapon-proof hide — equal odds, maximum glory. He posts himself as watch and trusts God’s favor; his men sleep.

Grendel comes in off the moors through mist, rips the door, kills one Geat for a snack, then reaches for Beowulf. Bad move. Beowulf’s handgrip locks on. The hall nearly shakes apart in the struggle. Grendel’s strength fails; Beowulf tears the monster’s **arm and shoulder** clean off. Grendel flees to the fen to die. At dawn the arm hangs under Heorot’s roof as a trophy — proof the boast was kept and the hall is “purged.”

**Voice note (light):** The stalker approach is pure battle-horror music; the close often adds “God-cursed / glory granted” language so victory reads as both athletic wreck and spiritual cleanup.

## Block C — Lines 1159–1250  
### Victory feast wind-down → Wealhtheow’s gifts → bed (false safety)

**What happens**

*(This is the **after-Grendel** party winding down — not the pre-fight bedtime scene around 640–691.)*

After the Finnsburg entertainment lay, the hall is still celebrating. **Wealhtheow** moves through with the cup, speeches, and a precious **torque** (neck-ring) for Beowulf. Under the glitter, she’s managing anxiety about succession and about Hrothgar’s nephew **Hrothulf** — gifts and kind words try to glue the royal family together. The narrator quietly flashes forward: that same glory-object will one day ride with Hygelac toward a fatal raid. Bright gifts already contain a funeral.

Wealhtheow then charges Beowulf to be kindly toward her sons — turning the foreign monster-killer into a guardian of Danish hearth-boys. Fame is supposed to serve the house, not only the hero’s name.

Night falls. Benches become bedding. Warriors sleep with mail and weapons ready — “a right people.” The poem hints that one man is marked for death. Victory has made them *less* sharp than Beowulf’s first-night vigil. Comfort is the setup for the next attack.

**Voice note (light):** Mostly hall etiquette and fate-irony here; less sermon, more “grim shape of things to come.”

## Block D — Lines 1251–1650  
### Grendel’s mother → mere → fight → head back to Heorot

**What happens**

While the Danes sleep, **Grendel’s mother** raids to avenge her son. She grabs **Aeschere**, Hrothgar’s right-hand man, and also takes Grendel’s trophy arm. Morning grief is personal and political — the king’s counselor is gone.

Hrothgar describes her lair: a haunted **mere** (lake) where even a hunted stag would rather die on the bank than jump in. He offers rewards if Beowulf will settle this new feud.

Beowulf’s answer is the fame-ethic in one line: better to **win glory before death** than mourn without action. The troop tracks blood to the cliff; Aeschere’s head is waiting there. Beowulf arms carefully. **Unferth** — who earlier mocked him — now loans the named sword **Hrunting**. Beowulf makes a bequest speech: if he dies, send his gear/gifts home to Hygelac; treat Unferth fairly.

He dives. She clutches him down into an underwater hall. Hrunting fails against her. She nearly kills him; his mail and — the narrator insists — **God tipping the balance** save him. He spots an ancient **giant-made sword**, beheads her, then finds Grendel’s corpse and beheads that too. The blade melts from hot blood; only the ornate **hilt** survives. Four men haul Grendel’s huge head back to Heorot. The drinking-hall stares; private ordeal becomes public proof again.

**Voice note (light):** Hard braid underwater — warrior sport *and* “holy God decided.” Kin-vengeance makes the mother feel almost justified *and* demonic at once; the poem won’t flatten her to one label.

## Block E — Lines 1799–2199  
### Homeward: farewell, sail, report, gifts up to Hygelac

**What happens**

Morning after the mother-feast: Beowulf wants to go home to Hygelac. He returns Hrunting to Unferth **without blame** — courtesy as a fame practice. He promises Denmark future spear-help if enemies ever attack.

**Hrothgar** praises him, half-prophesies he’ll make a great Geat king someday, loads him with **twelve treasures**, and weeps at the goodbye. Perfect meetings still end; hall joy is temporary.

They sail. Coast-guard gets a thank-you sword. Treasure is unloaded into Geatland. Queen **Hygd** is introduced as open-handed (contrast material about a bad queen sits nearby in the poem). In Hygelac’s hall Beowulf doesn’t just brag — he also analyzes Danish politics: Hrothgar’s plan to marry daughter **Freawaru** to Heatho-Bard prince **Ingeld** will probably fail when some old spearman spots an heirloom sword and feud memory restarts mid-feast. Wisdom, not only muscle, is on display for his own lord.

Then Beowulf retells the fights (including the Geat **Hondscio**, whom Grendel ate), hands Danish treasure **upward** to Hygelac and a neck-ornament to Hygd — proper thane behavior: glory recirculates; you don’t keep the credit solo. Hygelac rewards him with land, a hall, and a sword. Uncle and nephew honor each other; hierarchy holds. The torque’s path (Wealhtheow → Beowulf → Hygd) finishes a gift-sentence that started in Denmark.

*(Dragon material starts ~2200 — outside this homeward block.)*

**Voice note (light):** Departure and sail feel bright and oral; the Freawaru warning is worldly feud-realism; closing gifts mix reciprocity ethics with a flicker of “God-sent strength.”

## How the five blocks connect (one skim paragraph)

Shield’s ideal king → Hrothgar’s broken hall → Beowulf’s foreign fix (handgrip, arm trophy) → celebration that goes soft → mother’s kin-revenge resets the debt → deeper win (head + hilt) → homeward ethics: report honestly, give upward, take land. Monster-killing is only half the story; **speech, gifts, and loyalty loops** are the other half. Dual voice keeps asking whether the afterlife that matters is **Christian salvation** or **pagan name-glory** — often in the same scene.
